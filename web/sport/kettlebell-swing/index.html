How to do a perfect kettlebell swing

A kettlebell swing is not a squat and it is not a front raise — it is a hip hinge with an explosion at the end. Hike the bell back between your legs, snap the hips forward, and let the bell float to chest height on momentum alone. Done right it trains the glutes, hamstrings and core while spiking the heart rate like a sprint. The four checkpoints

Every bad swing fails at one of these four points. The callouts track them while the athlete swings.

Hinge

The hips travel back, not down. Knees stay soft with the shins still — the moment they drift forward, the swing has become a squat and the quads have stolen the rep from the hamstrings.

Spine

Neutral and braced the whole way. At the bottom the bell pulls the torso forward, and the abs and lower back have to hold the line — a rounded back under a swinging load is where tweaks come from.

Float

The bell rises to chest height on hip power alone and genuinely floats at the top. Finish as a standing plank — glutes squeezed, ribs down, no leaning back — then let it fall; never hold it up with the shoulders.

Grip

Both hands hooked through the handle, shoulders packed down and back. The grip holds tight while the arms stay loose — tight arms turn the swing into a front raise and rob the hips of their snap.

The swing, step by step

The full sequence plays on the stage — the athlete hikes the bell in, then swings on loop. Drop it to half speed to watch the hinge.

1

Set up

Bell on the floor a foot in front of you. Hinge down with a flat back, grip the handle with both hands, and tip the bell toward you so the lats switch on before anything moves.

2

Hike it back

Pull the bell back between the legs like a football snap — high toward the hips, not low toward the knees — until the forearms brush the inner thighs and the wrists meet the mid-thigh.

3

Snap the hips

Drive the hips forward hard and stand tall in one motion. The bell floats up on that snap alone — if you are raising it with the shoulders, the rep belongs to the arms, not the hips.

4

Lockout

At the top you are a standing plank: glutes squeezed, abs braced, ribs down, stacked over the heels. Take one breath at the top if you need it — never lean back into the arch.

5

Reload

Let the bell fall and be patient: wait until the forearms re-contact the thighs, then hinge sharply and fire the next rep. No pause at the top, no racing the bell down.

Muscles the kettlebell swing works

Tap the muscle chips above — or the labels on the small stage — to light each one up on the athlete.

Glutes the engine

The hip snap that floats the bell is a maximal glute squeeze at lockout. Every rep is a hip thrust delivered at speed.

Hamstrings the spring

Loaded hard on the backswing as the hips fold back, then recoiling into the snap like a stretched band. The stretch you feel at the bottom is the rep working.

Core the brace

Abs lock the ribcage over the pelvis so the bell cannot fold you forward at the bottom — or tempt you to arch back at the top.

Lats the steering

Lats and upper back keep the bell close and high on the backswing, instead of letting it sink low between the knees where the lower back takes over.

Grip the connection

The forearms hold the handle shut for the whole set while the arms themselves stay relaxed. High-rep swings are quietly one of the best grip builders there is.

Shoulders the guides

Delts stay packed down and merely guide the arc. If the shoulders burn before the glutes do, the swing has become an arm lift — reset the hips and start over.

Russian vs American — and how to programme it

Same hip snap, two finish lines. The Russian swing stops at chest height; the American swing continues overhead. Almost everyone should earn the second with the first.

Russian swing — to chest height

The standard. The bell floats to chest or eye level and falls back under control, which keeps the load in the hips and the spine neutral. Start here regardless of experience — the overhead finish adds range the shoulders often pay for before the hips do.

Progression rule: when every set stays crisp with two reps in reserve, size the bell up. Never chase weight past the point where the hip snap dies — a slow swing is a different exercise.

American swing — overhead

CrossFit's variant: the same hip drive, continued to a full overhead lockout. The hips still do the lifting — but the last quarter of the range belongs to the shoulders, and a tired overhead finish is where rounded backs and lazy reps live. Move to it only once chest-height swings float without effort.

Common WOD doses run 10–20 reps per round or 40–50 unbroken as a finisher. Whatever the dose, the checkpoints are identical: hinge back, snap through, let the bell travel — and the moment the bell needs an arm lift to arrive, the set is over whether the whiteboard says so or not.

One more cousin worth knowing: the single-arm swing. Same pattern, one hand, and the anti-rotation demand on the core doubles. Alternate sides each set — it exposes the side that has been coasting.

Mistakes that steal the snap

Four errors turn a hip-powered swing into an arm-powered lift — and all of them are visible the moment you look for them.

Squatting the swing

The most common fault of all: the knees travel forward, the bell dips low between the shins, and the quads take over. Fix it by pushing the hips back further and keeping the bell high between the thighs on the backswing.

Lifting with the arms

If the bell stalls below chest height or the shoulders ache first, the arms are raising it instead of riding it. Keep them as ropes and let the hips throw the bell — the float at the top should feel free.

Rounding the back at the bottom

A rounded spine under a swinging load is where back tweaks come from. Chest proud, abs braced, back flat through the hinge — and if the hinge can't be held flat, the bell is too heavy.

Leaning back at the top

Overextending into an arch swaps a flat-back finish for a compressed one. Finish tall and stacked — glutes and abs doing the locking, ribs down, the bell floating in front of a vertical torso.

The swing shares its engine with the carry: both live or die on the hips staying tall under load. See the same brace tested over distance in the farmer's carry form guide, or the hinge loaded front-to-back in the back squat.
