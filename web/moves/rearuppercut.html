How to throw a rear uppercut

The knockout punch from below — the rear hand drives up through the chin with the whole body behind it.

Punch #6 Rear hand Fight ender Comes from below the guard

Action

Bend the knees and load the rear side, then explode upward: the rear leg extends, the hip turns over, and the rear fist travels in a tight vertical line under the guard to the chin, palm facing you, elbow tucked and pointing down. The lead hand stays high at the cheek. Land, then bring the fist straight back to the guard — do not let it hang in the air.

When to use

The classic finisher after the head comes down: behind a body shot, when the opponent is rocked and sagging forward, or as the last punch of a combination like 1-2-body-6. It is also a natural answer to an opponent who ducks under your straight punches.

Advantages

The most destructive single punch in the basic set — the force travels up through the legs, hip and back into the chin, the one target that turns the lights out. It also comes from below the guard, where most fighters keep almost no defence.

Limits

It needs distance control and a set-up: thrown from too far it becomes a slow swing that a hook beats easily. You drop your level to throw it, your head moves into hook range, and if it misses there is a long recovery with the rear side wide open.

Common mistakes

Everything the lead uppercut can do subtly wrong, the rear uppercut does loudly. The full torso dip to load the shot is a broadcast, not a tell. Swinging outward instead of driving up through the target turns it into an arc the defender simply steps away from. And because the rear hand carries the body’s rotation, leaving it low on the recovery is the single most counter-inviting habit in boxing — the opponent’s lead hook is already loaded against it. Never chase a retreating opponent with it; it needs a still target.

How defenders react

Defenders treat the rear uppercut as a punch you make them hold still for. They catch it on the open palm with the wrist turned down, step straight back and jab the recovery, or roll under it and answer with a hook to the body while the rear arm is still extended. Timing beats power here: the punch is slower to arrive than the cross, so a defender who is moving on the half-beat never has to deal with it at all.
