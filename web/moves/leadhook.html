How to throw a lead hook

A short horizontal arc from the lead hand that goes around the guard instead of through it.

Punch #3 Lead hand Close range Comes around the guard

Action

Keep the elbow bent at roughly 90 degrees and level with the fist. From the guard, pivot the lead foot and turn the whole lead side of the body: the hip rotates, the shoulder swings around, and the fist travels in a tight horizontal arc toward the jaw, knuckles facing down. The arm stays rigid — it does not swing. The power is body rotation, not the shoulder. Bring the fist back inside your guard immediately.

When to use

At close to mid range, when the guard is high and tight: the hook goes around it, not through it. Classic placements are after the jab (1-3) or after the cross (1-2-3), aimed at the jaw or the temple — or, with the elbow slightly wider, at the ribs and liver.

Advantages

It attacks from outside the opponent's field of vision, so it arrives where the guard isn't. With the whole body behind the rotation it is one of the two classic knockout punches, and it needs almost no wind-up.

Limits

It only works at the right distance: too far and it becomes a slap, too close and there is no room for the arc. A wide miss swings your own jaw into the counter, and the punch telegraphs badly if the elbow flares before the hip turns.

Common mistakes

The arm swing is the giveaway: a hook thrown as a wide arm sweep, without the hips turning, arrives as a slap and leaves the shoulder exposed. The elbow should stay roughly level with the wrist so the fist, wrist and elbow form one line at impact. Dropping the lead hand to wind up is the second killer — the opponent’s rear hand is already on its way. Finally, leaning the head across the centreline to reach the target puts your chin directly on their rear-cross lane.

How defenders react

Against a hook the defender raises the lead elbow and forearm to block, covers the ear with the glove and answers with a rear straight, or shells up completely and walks forward through it. The punch only lands when it is hidden — fired tight behind the jab, off a slipped cross, or when the guard has been pulled down with a body shot. A hook that starts from outside the opponent’s vision arrives; one that starts in front of their eyes gets blocked.
