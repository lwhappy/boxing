How to throw a jab

The fastest punch in boxing: lead hand, straight line, back to the chin before the opponent sees it.

Punch #1 Lead hand Long range Starts every combo

Action

Start from your stance with the lead foot forward and the weight even on both legs. From the guard the lead fist travels in a straight line from your shoulder to the target: the elbow stays directly under the fist, the rear hand stays glued to the chin, the chin tucks behind the lead shoulder and the rear shoulder is slightly higher than the lead one. The fist snaps back along the same path the instant it lands — it should return faster than it went out. No wind-up, almost no hip turn.

When to use

The measuring stick of boxing: throw it to read distance, to occupy the opponent's eyes, to interrupt their rhythm, to make them flinch, and to pull the guard open so the rear hand has a gap to shoot through. Also the safest way to keep a taller opponent from walking in.

Advantages

The shortest travel time of any punch, so it is the hardest to see and the cheapest to recover from. It keeps you balanced and in stance, it can be thrown while moving in any direction, and it is the entry point of almost every combination. A fast jab wins rounds on its own because it scores without exposing you.

Limits

Power is low by design — it does not stop a pressure fighter who is willing to eat it, and judges rarely reward it as a damaging blow. If it is thrown lazy with the hand dropping on the way back, it hands the opponent a free counter over the top; if the lead shoulder stays low, the chin is exposed behind it.

Common mistakes

The two errors that cost most: the hand dropping on the way back — it telegraphs the next exchange and leaves the chin naked over the top — and reaching for distance by leaning in, which takes the weight off the back foot so nothing follows. Also watch the elbow: it must travel under the fist in a straight line. An elbow that wings outward is slower, easier to see, and punishes the shoulder over a long session. And remember to breathe out on the snap; a held breath fades the jab after two rounds.

How defenders react

A competent opponent does one of three things with your jab: catches it on the glove and answers over the top with a rear counter, slips outside the line and returns a cross down the middle, or pulls back half a step and out-jabs you on the recovery. Every answer punishes a slow return hand — which is why the snap back matters more than the snap out. Vary the height (head, chest, guard) and the rhythm so the defence can never settle on one answer.
